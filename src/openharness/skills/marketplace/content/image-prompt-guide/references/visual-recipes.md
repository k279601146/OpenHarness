# Visual Design Recipes

Use these as optional decisions for a specific message. Follow supplied brand guidelines first. No preset overrides exact copy, product identity, user style, or destination requirements.

| Communication need | Useful treatment | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Clear technical explanation | Restrained sans serif, aligned labels, one focal detail or diagram | Keep label links accurate; avoid decorative pseudo-data |
| Material or craft story | Close-up texture, quiet typography, directional light revealing the surface | Show the actual material, not a premium substitute |
| Promotional impact | Readable bold display type, selective dimensional lettering or contrast | Give conditions room; avoid unrelated fine print and invented urgency |
| Warm everyday use | Natural setting, relaxed composition, approachable type | Preserve true product color and credible contact |
| Editorial brand message | Deliberate whitespace and a small number of typographic levels | Do not force a fixed font count, color palette or density percentage |

## Typography as a local design choice

Choose letterform, stroke character, material/effect and placement to support the message. A graphic may replace part of a display letter only if the word remains recognizable. Use expressive treatment for a short headline; keep prices, conditions and body copy easy to read. Validate actual glyphs and script support for native rendering. Do not transfer sample dates, copyright lines, slogans or brand names with a typography reference.

For multi-script artwork, maintain hierarchy without forcing identical physical font sizes across scripts. Respect right-to-left reading and do not reverse numbers or brand identifiers manually.

Use a concrete type decision instead of "high-end typography": **letterform -> weight/contrast -> placement -> optional effect**. For example, a short promotional headline can use broad bold letters in a clear text region, while eligibility text uses a simpler regular face beneath it. For an editorial message, a restrained serif headline and aligned caption may work; neither is a rule for an industry or country.

Prompt clause: "Render '[exact headline]' in [concrete letterform/weight], aligned at [location]. Keep '[supporting copy]' subordinate but readable. Apply [requested effect] only to [display lettering/background], preserving every letter and leaving [product/conditions] unobscured." Name a specific font only when supplied, appropriate and supported; for native rendering verify availability and glyph coverage.

Do not let ornamental stroke substitutions, bevels, metallic shine or condensed letters make a price, unit, qualifier or product name ambiguous. A designed word image does not deliver an installable typeface.

## Material and production cues

Paper may use printed, embossed or foil details; fabric may use embroidery or woven labels; metal may use engraving. Select a plausible application for the requested mockup and asset. These are visual concept treatments, not guarantees of manufacturing feasibility. Reuse approved logos without redesigning them during application.

## Using a supplied visual reference

Extract the relevant mechanism: hierarchy, spacing, camera placement, lighting or type treatment. Bind that reference as style/layout only. Keep useful specific guidance, but discard unrelated sample facts, image-model buzzwords, arbitrary camera settings and conflicting blanket prohibitions. Prefer one appropriate reference over a collection of loosely matched styles.

For a requested concept effect, resolve the **effect target** before prompting: background, decoration, display text, mascot, or the merchandise itself. A paper-cut scene behind a real product does not authorize turning the product into paper. Specify the target and protected elements in the prompt. Do not introduce a surprise material transformation into a normal commercial asset.
